# People need Idle: written before leaving the job.

![A photo taken not long after starting at the job in Shenzhen at sunset ](/images/pictures/41c9e9b45e09d1fbf8c62050b5638051bccf9e93b3a5a55eefc9d0e862bf3347.webp)

This photo was taken on May 14th, shortly after starting. That day, I saw the sunset reflecting on the buildings in Shenzhen, with a thick orange-red sky. Suddenly, I had many hopes for this city and the future life ahead.

At that time, I thought this would be a very good job. The project is interesting, AI is changing daily, and many things don’t have fixed answers yet. It’s perfect for using a bit of youthful enthusiasm and free will to move forward.

Looking back now, I don't think I misread anything at the time. This job still has its advantages, and I have indeed accomplished many things I couldn't do before here. However, the energy in those photos seems a bit weakened by now.

Recently I have been writing up the handover for my departure from the job.

At first, I managed to gather over a thousand lines, but after reading them, I had only one feeling: it's not enough. I worked on this project for five months in a row—how could I possibly finish it with just over a thousand lines? So I continued to add more content, documenting interfaces, pages, states, deployment, configurations, and all the pitfalls I encountered. Eventually, the Markdown file grew to over four thousand six hundred lines.

Only when it reached this length did I take a step back from the work for the first time to see clearly what had happened over these months.

Since April, I have been working on an AI math education video project. Though called development, I've done a lot of things: front-end, back-end, model invocation, Agent, task queue, voice, rendering, deployment, monitoring, and after issues occur online, reassembling clues scattered across databases, logs, and servers.

Over these five months, I have also gradually changed my understanding of AI, Agents, and work.

## In April, I started it off.

In April, things progressed quickly.

The code generated by the model is truncated, so we continue writing the continuation; formula rendering fails, so we perform additional preprocessing; video duration is incorrect, so we add planning steps; tasks seem to be in queue, but in reality it might just be a broken database connection. Voice, images, payments, permissions, the management backend, and deployment are also all progressing at the same rate.

My most immediate feeling about AI at that time was: fast.

It can read a lot of code, understand both front and backends simultaneously, and quickly turn a vague requirement into something executable. In the past, multiple days of revisions might be needed, but now many iterations can be done in a single day. Once problems arise, I have become accustomed to continuing to hand over logs and code to Codex, for it to analyze, modify, and verify again.

This speed is very exciting. It seems that as long as the model is stronger, the context is longer, and the prompt is more complete, many engineering issues will disappear naturally.

Later, I realized that AI has indeed increased development speed, but it also accelerates chaos as well.

A requirement with unclear boundaries can be turned into code more quickly; a judgment without a factual source can spread across the entire system more rapidly; a problem that should have been fixed locally might also be rewritten by it enthusiastically. Speed itself does not decide for people what to do, nor does it bear the final outcome for the system.

## The presence of an Agent does not mean it actually does things.

In June, I asked a very direct question: Is LangGraph still useful? Why don’t we feel the impact of a strong Agent?

After checking, the answer is a bit awkward. It is indeed online, the nodes are running, and events are being generated. From a program structure perspective, it can definitely be called an Agent. But what users actually see is more like a workflow with a model: generation, inspection, repair, and try again until the budget runs out or a lucky outcome occurs.

What concerns me even more is that for the same failed task, Codex can be fixed manually through manual troubleshooting, yet the Agent in the system often loops endlessly.

The difference isn’t just the model.

When fixing manually, I first confirm where the failure occurred: what is the status in the database, what stage the event has reached, whether the generated code is complete, what errors appear in the rendering logs, and whether the final video is actually produced. After finding the real breakpoint, I only modify the smallest part and verify independently once more.

At that time, the Agent had many checkers, fixers, and retry budgets, but lacked a unified decision-making path. It would take actions, but doesn’t necessarily know which action is worth doing; it receives errors, but may not be able to distinguish whether they are code issues, tool issues, model issues, or simply tasks that have already been completed with the status not properly recorded.

This changed my understanding of Agents.

An Agent isn’t just putting a large model into a loop and adding a few tools. A truly useful Agent needs to know where the facts are, see what it just did, receive accurate feedback after failure, and know when to stop and hand over the problem to deterministic rules or humans.

So-called autonomy doesn’t mean constant action. Being able to restrain oneself is also a skill.

## From "letting AI write" to "letting AI be responsible for results"

By July and August, most of my work is no longer simply about having AI generate code.

We have tried allowing Agent to read and edit files natively, having two Reviewer check them separately, then Coder modify them, and finally re-render and check them; we also break down the knowledge in geometry, functions, and calculation problems into twenty-five small Prompt Skills, which can be combined when needed.

These experiments have made me more and more convinced that context is not the more the better, nor are rules the longer the better.

Putting all experiences into a single large prompt makes it easy for the model to get distracted. A more effective approach is to break tasks into clear, small capabilities: when to read, where changes are allowed, what facts to use for judgment, and what must be rechecked after modification. Each part is small, but they can be combined, replaced, and reused.

This isn’t fundamentally different from writing regular software. We still need to define interfaces, states, and responsibilities, just with some executors becoming AI.

I no longer trust the word “success” easily.

A 200 response from the interface doesn’t mean the feature is actually usable; a service showing as active doesn’t mean it’s running new code; queue confirmation doesn’t mean tasks have been delivered; a SUCCESS in the database doesn’t mean the video can play; having an MP4 on the server doesn’t mean the user gets this version.

For this project, true completion requires verification throughout to the final product: code version, running process, task status, file identity, video information, Range request, and finally, actual playback.

The more capable AI becomes, the more specific we need to define "completion". Otherwise, it will only work extremely hard to achieve a wrong goal.

## Later, I became that main thread.

The project gradually stabilized, and ordinary tasks could be produced in batches, reaching the stage of preparation for delivery and further expansion. Logically, I should feel a bit easier than at the beginning.

But my experience is the opposite.

We need to maintain the problem-solving pipeline, modify the frontend, monitor the server, track customer feedback, and temporary requirements will intersperse. If any part fails, it seems to end up with me. I often feel like a main thread in Node.js, continuously receiving events and undergoing Context Switch.

If the queue in the system becomes congested, we examine concurrency, backpressure, and timeouts; if the machine runs out of capacity, we increase capacity, implement rate limiting, and schedule retries. But when it comes to humans, it seems that as long as they can respond, tasks are allowed to be added.

But humans need Idle time.

Idling is not laziness. Without downtime, the mind can't quickly organize what just happened, nor can it enter a complete state of thought. Many truly important decisions aren't made when messages keep popping up. It requires one or two hours without interruption, sometimes even a complete lack of desire to work after work hours.

On my birthday in July, I was busy until 11:40 PM. My mother asked where I was going to play that day, and only then did I realize it was my birthday. Later, while walking in Liuxiandong, I looked up at office buildings lit one by one, and for the first time, I saw the "work atmosphere" of Shenzhen in detail.

I wrote all these things in my blog at that time. Looking back now, they were actually reminding me long ago: work has become more than just occupying time; it has started to take away my ability to feel time.

## Why did I decide to leave?

It’s not this project itself that I want to leave.

I still enjoy turning AI capabilities into truly operational, deliverable systems. Rather than focusing on a demo, I care more about how tasks are restored, who is responsible for the state, how evidence is preserved after an error occurs, and whether the final outcome actually reaches the user. These are still the aspects I want to continue working on.

What I want to leave is another issue: having one person act as the scheduler for the entire project over the long term.

I hope in my next role, responsibilities can be clearer. I want to focus on maintaining a core link, with a unified requirement entry point, someone to arrange priorities, and others to handle other directions. Of course, there will still be pressure, and it won’t automatically become easier just because I switch organizations, but at least I want to confirm that I’m working with a team, not another single-threaded cycle with a changed name.

Leaving isn't entirely a negation.

Over five months, this project has gone from continuous trial and error to a stage where it can produce, review, and deliver results. I started organizing the roles that new employees take over, wrote that交接 document with over 4,600 lines, and tried to turn things that were only known to me into paths that others can follow.

Being able to deliver results is also part of the project's maturity.

## Written during these days before leaving

I haven’t completely left yet. There are documents still to be corrected in the computer, versions to be confirmed on the server, and some issues too. I know they won’t disappear immediately just because I submit my resignation.

Just the mindset has changed a bit.

Before, I always thought that if I fixed this issue and completed that link, the project would finally reach a relaxed state. Now I know that there will always be the next event in the system. The real boundary cannot wait for events to disappear on their own; it must be chosen by humans.

Over these five months, I’ve learned how to let AI write production code, how to use agents with tools, and how to confirm with evidence that a task has truly been completed.

As I am about to leave my job, I also want to learn something more ordinary: not to treat myself as a service that must remain online forever.

The system can run continuously.

People should have occasional downtime.

I also hope that when I encounter such a sunset again next time, I will still have the mood to stop and take a photo. Not because the next job will definitely be better, but just because I hope that that somewhat childlike freedom will gradually return.

Love you always.
