# Need for Idle: Written before leaving the job

![Photo of sunset in Shenzhen taken shortly after joining the company](/images/pictures/41c9e9b45e09d1fbf8c62050b5638051bccf9e93b3a5a55eefc9d0e862bf3347.webp)

This photo was taken on May 14th, just after joining the company. That day, I saw the sunset casting a rich orange-red color on the buildings in Shenzhen, and suddenly I had many hopes for this city and the future life ahead.

At that time, I thought this would be a very good job. The project is interesting, AI is changing daily, and many things don’t have fixed answers yet—it’s perfect for using youthful enthusiasm and free will to move forward.

Looking back now, I don't think I misread anything at the time. This job still has its advantages, and I have indeed accomplished many things I couldn't do before here. However, the energy in those photos seems a bit subdued by now.

Recently I have been writing up the handover for my departure from the job.

At first, I managed to gather over a thousand lines, but after reading them, I had only one feeling: it wasn't enough. I worked on this project for five months in a row—how could I possibly finish it with just over a thousand lines? So I continued adding more, writing down the interfaces, pages, states, deployment, configurations, and all the pitfalls I encountered. Eventually, the Markdown file reached over four thousand six hundred lines.

Only when it reached this length did I take a step back from the work for the first time to see clearly what had actually happened over these months.

From April until now, I have been working on an AI math education video project. Though it’s called development, I’ve done a lot: front-end, back-end, model invocation, Agent, task queue, voice, rendering, deployment, monitoring, and when issues occur online, piecing together clues scattered across databases, logs, and servers.

Over these five months, I have also gradually changed my understanding of AI, Agents, and work.

## April: Let it run first

In April, things progressed rapidly.

The code generated by the model is truncated, so we add the missing parts; formula rendering fails, so we perform additional preprocessing; video duration is incorrect, so we add planning steps; tasks seem to be in queue, but in reality it might just be a broken database connection. Voice, images, payments, permissions, the management backend, and deployment are also all progressing at the same rate.

The most immediate feeling I have about AI is speed.

It can read a lot of code, understand both front and backends simultaneously, and quickly turn a vague requirement into something executable. In the past, multiple days of revisions might be needed, but now many iterations can be done in a single day. Once issues arise, I also tend to continue handing over logs and code to Codex to analyze, modify, and verify further.

This speed is very exciting. It seems that if the model is stronger, the context longer, and the prompt more complete, many engineering problems will disappear naturally.

Later, I realized that AI has indeed increased development speed, but it also accelerates all the chaos together.

A request with unclear boundaries can be turned into code more quickly; a judgment without a factual source can spread across the entire system more rapidly; a problem that should have been fixed locally might also be rewritten enthusiastically by it. Speed itself does not decide for people what to do, nor does it bear the final results for the system.

## The presence of an Agent does not mean it actually does things

In June, I asked a very direct question: Is LangGraph still useful? Why don’t we feel the impact of a strong Agent?

After checking, the answer is a bit awkward. It is indeed online, the nodes are running, and events are being generated. From a program structure perspective, it can definitely be called an Agent. However, what users actually see is more like a workflow with a model: generation, inspection, repair, and retrying until the budget runs out or a lucky outcome occurs.

What concerns me even more is that for the same failed task, Codex can be fixed manually through manual troubleshooting, but the Agent in the system often loops back on the same steps.

The difference isn’t just the model.

When fixing manually, I first confirm where the failure occurs: what the database looks like, what stage the event has reached, whether the generated code is complete, what the rendering logs report, and whether the final video is actually produced. After finding the real breakpoint, I only modify the smallest part and verify independently once more.

At that time, the Agent had many checkers, fixers, and retry budgets, but lacked a unified decision-making path. It would take actions, but doesn’t necessarily know which action is worth doing; it receives errors, but may not be able to distinguish whether the issue is code-related, tool-related, model-related, or because the task was already completed, only the status isn’t correctly recorded back.

This changed my understanding of Agents.

An Agent isn’t just putting a large model into a loop with a few tools. A truly useful Agent needs to know where the facts are, can see what it just did, gets accurate feedback after failure, and knows when to stop and hand over the problem to deterministic rules or humans.

So-called autonomy doesn’t mean acting continuously. Being able to restrain oneself is also a skill.

## From "Letting AI Write" to "Letting AI Be Responsible for Results"

By July and August, most of my work no longer involves simply having AI generate code.

We have tried allowing Agent to read and edit files natively, having two Reviewer check them separately, then Coder modify them, and finally re-render and check them; we also break down the skills from geometry, functions, and calculation problems into twenty-five small Prompts Skills, which can be combined when needed.

These experiments have made me more and more convinced that context is not the more the better, nor are rules the longer the better.

Putting all experiences into a single large prompt makes it easy for the model to get distracted. A more effective approach is to break tasks into clear, small capabilities: when to read, where changes are allowed, which facts to use for judgment, and what must be rechecked after modification. Each part is small, but they can be combined, replaced, and reused.

This isn’t fundamentally different from writing regular software. We still need to define interfaces, states, and responsibilities, just with some executors becoming AI.

I no longer easily believe the word “success.”

A 200 response from the interface doesn’t mean the feature is actually usable; a service showing as active doesn’t mean it’s running new code; queue confirmation doesn’t mean the task has been delivered; a SUCCESS result in the database doesn’t mean the video can play; having an MP4 on the server doesn’t mean the user gets this version.

For this project, true completion requires verification throughout the entire process: code version, running process, task status, file identity, video information, Range request, and finally, actual playback.

The more capable AI becomes, the more specific we need to define what "completion" means. Otherwise, it will only work extremely hard to achieve a wrong goal.

## Later on, I became that main thread.

The project gradually stabilized, and ordinary tasks could be produced in batches. It also reached the stage of preparation for delivery and further expansion. Logically, I should feel a bit easier than at the beginning.

But my feelings are exactly the opposite.

We need to maintain the problem-solving pipeline, make changes to the frontend, monitor the server, track customer feedback, and temporary requirements will intersperse. If any part fails, it seems to end up with me. I often feel like a main thread in Node.js, continuously receiving events and undergoing Context Switch.

If the queue in the system becomes congested, we examine concurrency, backpressure, and timeouts; when the machine runs out of capacity, we increase capacity, implement rate limiting, and arrange retries. But when it comes to humans, it seems that as long as they can respond, tasks are allowed to be added continuously.

But humans need Idle time.

Idle is not laziness. Without downtime, the mind can't quickly organize what just happened, nor can it enter a full thought process. Many truly important decisions aren't made when messages keep popping up. It requires one or two hours without interruption, sometimes even a complete lack of work after work hours.

On my birthday in July, I was busy until 11:40 PM. My mother asked where I was going to play that day, and only then did I realize it was my birthday. Later, while walking in Liuxiandong, I looked up at office buildings lit one by one, and for the first time I saw the "work atmosphere" of Shenzhen in detail.

I wrote all these things in my blog at that time. Looking back now, they were actually reminding me long ago: work has become more than just occupying time; it has started to take away my ability to feel time.

## Why I decided to leave

It’s not this project itself that I want to leave.

I still enjoy turning AI capabilities into real, operational systems that can be delivered. Rather than focusing on a demo, I care more about how tasks are restored, who is responsible for the state, how evidence is preserved after an exception occurs, and whether the final outcome actually reaches the users. These are still the aspects I want to continue working on.

What I want to leave is another issue: having one person act as the scheduler for the entire project over the long term.

I hope in my next role, responsibilities can be more clearly defined. I want to focus on maintaining a core link, with a unified entry point for requirements, someone to arrange priorities, and others to handle other directions. Of course, there will still be pressure, and it won’t automatically become easier just because I switch organizations, but at least I want to confirm that I’m working with a team, not another single-threaded cycle with a changed name.

Leaving is not entirely a negation.

Over five months, this project has gone from continuous trial and error to a stage where it can produce, review, and deliver results. I started organizing the roles that new employees need to take over, writing that交接 document with over 4,600 lines, trying to turn things that were only known to me into paths that others can follow.

Being able to deliver results is also part of the project's maturity.

## Notes from these few days before leaving

I haven’t completely left yet. There are documents still to be reviewed in the computer, versions to be confirmed on the server, and some issues too. I know they won’t disappear immediately just because I submit my resignation.

It’s just that my mindset has changed slightly.

In the past, I always thought that if I could fix this issue and complete that connection, the project would finally reach a more relaxed state. Now I know that there will always be the next event in the system. The real boundary cannot wait for events to disappear on their own; it must be chosen by humans.

Over these five months, I’ve learned how to have AI write production code, how to use agents with tools, and how to confirm that a task is truly completed using evidence.

As I am about to leave my job, I also want to learn something more ordinary: not to treat myself as a service that must be online forever.

The system can run continuously.

People should have occasional downtime.

I also hope that when I encounter such a sunset again next time, I will still have the mood to stop and take a photo. It’s not because the next job will definitely be better, but just hoping that that somewhat naive freedom of will can return slowly.

Love you always.
