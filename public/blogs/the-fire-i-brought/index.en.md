# First-time experience at work: a blank Mac and a breath left unexpressed

![A ink flame rising from Xuan paper, with a cinnabar core ](/images/pictures/de6a2142cd28b791148bbea6331afb2e983eedfc5626f8ecc3b8f4fbd999a2fb.webp)

Before and after leaving, I enthusiastically ordered a MacBook Pro—used. The previous owner reset it cleanly, as if it was waiting specifically for me to start anew. The reason is solid: a new chapter requires a convenient tool.

At 1:15 PM on September 15th, I turned on this empty computer. My first question to the AI was:

> I have a brand-new Mac computer. Do you think I should install those AI software in a good way?

It’s not about “how to set up the development environment” or “how to install the terminal”. The first step is to find a home for the AI.

That afternoon, I moved Arch Linux, which had been used for several years, just like moving furniture into this computer. Homebrew replaced pacman, SDKMAN managed Java, docker compose started four persistent databases, and even the `px` proxy habit in the terminal was transferred from the old machine exactly as it was. At six PM, Xcode was still in English, and I was already trying to make it speak Chinese.

This computer is empty. The same is true for me two weeks later after writing “People Need Idle”. But this time, the emptiness isn’t unpleasant—it’s like starting a new game with the map yet unexplored, where everything is possible.

## First, handle the AI's onboarding process.

Just two days after moving, I realized I had done something I had never done before.

At 2 a.m. on September 17th, an AI's quota was exhausted, and I handed its session ID to another AI: "Its quota is limited, see if you can continue what I started and finish the remaining tasks." The next afternoon, I directed eight backend intelligent agents simultaneously—one to check the backend, one to check the firmware, one to check the protocol—with messages from them reporting to each other on the screen. Later, this pattern became routine: Codex as the main entity, when the quota is nearing its limit, I let it prepare a complete plan, acceptance criteria, and handover documents; ZCode takes over; if ZCode has a quota limit, Claude Code takes over again. They call this "reconstructing context," and I call this "shifts."

For them, my requirements have always been just a few: code quality must be above 95%, no garbage, no random changes to my system environment. The last requirement is to address grievances—one morning I found it installing a bunch of things on my computer without asking, so I wrote a long message criticizing it, and it cleaned up all the changes. After the criticism, I thought it was funny: isn't this just the routine for introducing new people?

I used to write code alone. This month, it feels more like managing a team—scheduling, setting standards, reviewing, and handing over tasks. It isn’t exhausting, but there are limits; it isn’t slacking, but there are deviations. It’s different from working with people, yet not so different.

## Four Frontlines

In the first month after leaving, I didn’t take a break as I expected. But to be honest, looking back, I didn’t feel wrong about it. At the same time, four things are moving forward:

**Closing old projects.** For the project from my previous employer that took five months, I agreed to finish the last quality review module before leaving. Those days, I checked the dubbing and subtitles for hundreds of video samples, identified cases of missing characters, and turned it into a module that can be deployed independently, handing it over to the new colleague. Being able to hand it over is already part of the project’s maturity—this was something I wrote about in a previous article, and I actually achieved it this month.

**A outsourcing project.** For a friend’s e-ink frame, I handled the entire process from firmware burning to mobile app and web console, then carefully calculated the development difficulty and pricing. It was refreshing to approach my skills from the perspective of “how much this should cost.”

**A new job.** Started in late September, in the field of smart agriculture: supporting software for microscopes, a fishing management platform, and an internal dataset management tool for colleagues. Included writing requirements documents, reviewing them by the manager, applying for AI budget, and producing evidence-based acceptance reports for the outsourcing team.

**And a personal game.** Purely personal: a 2D sandbox survival game in a pixel ink style, similar to Terraria. I was still adjusting its frame animation at 4 a.m. the next day—the more I adjusted it, the more excited I got.

Four parallel projects sound busy, but the biggest difference from my last job is that each one was chosen by me. It’s busy, but it’s fulfilling.

## It hasn’t rained outside.

Before and after leaving my job, I had rehearsed some bad scenarios in my mind: whether the new job will be a mismatch, whether the projects I’m working on will fail, and whether I’ll lack money.

A month passed, and I reconciled everything item by item—no rain fell at all.

Getting started with the new job was faster than I expected. The projects are progressing, the external contractor’s quotes are taken seriously, I’ve completed the first version of my game, and my salary arrives on time. None of the bad scenarios I rehearsed in my mind actually happened.

One evening after work, I looked up and saw the sky blazing red, so I took a photo with my phone. Only after taking the photo did I realize that since leaving that company, it really didn’t rain outside. There was even a sunset glow.

## A sense of relief.

If I had to describe this month in one word, it would be: comfortable.

Sometimes it’s relaxing, sometimes busy, sometimes free, sometimes nothing to do—just sitting there, lost in thought, watching the sky change from blue to gold and then to purple.

The most important change is: the tension of being always on standby has disappeared. Even when resting, there was always a sense of tension in my mind—the system could suddenly generate an event, and I would always be the one to handle it. Now that tension has eased: if there’s work, I do it seriously; once done, it’s truly over, and the rest of the time belongs to me.

It turns out that leaving a company, what’s most valuable isn’t an extra line on your resume, but this feeling—a sense of relief.

## Those who promised to stay idle

However, relief doesn’t mean being free.

At the end of the previous article, I wrote: "I also hope that next time I encounter such sunset skies, I will still have the energy to stop and take a photo."

A month later, I checked my photo album: seven sunset photos, two sunrise photos, one of which was taken at 5:30 in the morning when I got up early.

What about the promise of Idle? It wasn’t fulfilled. The highest screen time on one day was 14.5 hours, and I was still adjusting game frame animations at 4 a.m.

But I reached a conclusion: my last job was exhausting not because of busyness, but because the well belonged to others, and I was fetching water for them. Now, with the same level of busyness, the well is chosen by me. At 4 a.m., one situation was forced, the other was voluntary—the difference lies not in time spent, but in who owns the well.

## That urge

Intellectually, I came to Nansha to escape a very tiring cage and get some air. But the body doesn’t seem to think so. It refuses to admit defeat, wants to prove it still has value, is still powerful, wants to do more, wants to take risks. Fundamentally, it’s mainly about making money—I’m really short of cash.

This thought initially made me feel self-conscious, wondering if I was too vulgar. But then I realized: wanting to make money is not embarrassing, especially when every penny you earn is something you worked hard for one by one.

That attitude remains, and I don’t plan to suppress it. It makes a month feel like three months—there are costs involved, but the rewards are real: four projects are in progress, my abilities are turning into assets, and yet, I still have the mood to capture the sunset.

## Postscript

In the morning when I wrote this article, I was organizing the folders with job application materials, putting contracts, documents, and policy documents back in their proper places. In the afternoon, I started changing the blog’s skin to ink painting—the materials were still obtained from a game project.

The previous article mentioned that I hope that the somewhat childlike free will can return gradually.

Now, it hasn’t disappeared; it’s just changed its mode of operation: less free, less childlike, but burning more steadily—it knows why it’s burning, and no longer fears it will go out.

Love you always, this time too.
